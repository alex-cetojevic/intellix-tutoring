IB Physics Syllabus: What SL and HL Cover

Written by Alex Ćetojević

9 November 2026 · 9 min read

← Back to Blog
In short

IB Physics has five themes at both levels: A Space, time and motion; B The particulate nature of matter; C Wave behaviour; D Fields; and E Nuclear and quantum physics. SL has 110 hours of theory and HL has 180. HL adds five whole subtopics that SL never sees, including relativity and quantum physics, and goes deeper in most of the others. This course was first examined in 2025, so it is the one every current Diploma student is on.

The syllabus is the list of everything your Physics exams can ask. The IB's guide is long, and it is written for teachers, not for the person sitting the exam. This page gives you the same course in plain words: what each theme covers at SL, what HL adds, and which maths each part leans on. Use it to see what is coming, to find what you have not covered yet, or to help you choose between SL and HL.

In my lessons, the part students expect to be easy and then find hard is electromagnetism. It sits in Theme D, Fields.

Is this the syllabus you are on?

Yes, if your exams are in 2025 or later. The current course was first examined in 2025. The old course, with numbered topics and one option (relativity, engineering physics, imaging or astrophysics), had its last exams in November 2024.

This matters when you practise with past papers. Papers from 2024 and earlier are still useful, but the format was different, with a Paper 3, and some questions are on content that is no longer on the course. If an old question looks strange, check it against the lists below before you worry about it.

The five themes, and where HL's extra hours go

These are the teaching hours the IB suggests. Your school may spread them differently, but they show where the weight of the course is.

A: Space, time and motion2742+15
B: The particulate nature of matter2432+8

Three things stand out:

  • Fields doubles. It goes from 19 hours at SL to 38 at HL, the biggest jump of any theme.
  • Theme A is the largest at both levels. Forces, motion and energy come back in every other theme.
  • HL is SL plus more. Everything listed under SL below is on the HL course too.

Hours show teaching time. They do not tell you how many marks a theme gets on a paper. The lists below give the main content of each theme in my own words, not every line of the IB guide.

Theme A: Space, time and motion

SL: kinematics, with displacement, velocity and acceleration, the equations for constant acceleration, and projectiles (A.1); forces and momentum, with Newton's laws, free-body diagrams, friction, springs, circular motion, impulse and collisions (A.2); and work, energy and power, including efficiency (A.3).

HL adds: two whole subtopics. Rigid body mechanics (A.4) is the physics of things that turn: torque, moment of inertia and angular momentum. Galilean and special relativity (A.5) covers reference frames, the Lorentz transformation, time dilation, length contraction and space-time diagrams.

The maths it needs: splitting vectors into components, rearranging the constant acceleration equations, and reading a gradient or an area from a graph. A ball thrown sideways at 12 m/s from a 20 m cliff falls for about 2 seconds, because 20 = ½ × 9.8 × t2. So it lands about 24 m from the cliff. At HL, relativity is mostly algebra with one factor, γ. At 0.8c, 1 − 0.82 = 0.36, its square root is 0.6, and γ = 1/0.6 = 5/3. So 3 seconds on the moving clock last 5 seconds for you.

Theme B: The particulate nature of matter

SL: thermal energy transfers, with specific heat capacity, latent heat, conduction, convection and radiation from hot bodies (B.1); the greenhouse effect and the Earth's energy balance (B.2); the gas laws and the ideal gas (B.3); and current and circuits, with resistance, series and parallel resistors, and cells with internal resistance (B.5).

HL adds: thermodynamics (B.4): the first and second laws, internal energy, entropy and heat engines.

Electric circuits sit in this theme, not in Fields. If you look for them under D, you will not find them.

The maths it needs: proportion, and always working in kelvin. Heat a gas at constant volume from 27 °C to 327 °C and its pressure doubles, because 300 K becomes 600 K. Heat it from 20 °C to 40 °C and the pressure rises by less than 7%, not by 100%.

Theme C: Wave behaviour

SL: simple harmonic motion (C.1); the wave model, with transverse and longitudinal waves and v = fλ (C.2); reflection, refraction, diffraction and interference, including the double slit (C.3); standing waves on strings and in pipes, and resonance (C.4); and the Doppler effect (C.5).

HL adds: no new subtopic, but more depth in several: the equations of simple harmonic motion, single-slit diffraction and diffraction gratings, and the Doppler equations for sound.

The maths it needs: sine and cosine in radians, and angular frequency, ω = 2πf. A mass on a spring with a period of 0.5 s has ω = 2π ÷ 0.5 ≈ 12.6 rad/s. If your calculator is set to degrees, answers in this theme come out wrong.

Theme D: Fields

SL: gravitational fields, with Newton's law of gravitation, field strength and orbits (D.1); electric and magnetic fields, with Coulomb's law and field patterns (D.2); and how charged particles and current-carrying wires move in uniform fields (D.3).

HL adds: potential and potential energy in gravitational and electric fields, escape speed, and a whole subtopic on electromagnetic induction (D.4): magnetic flux, Faraday's law, Lenz's law and generators.

The maths it needs: inverse square laws and powers of ten. Double your distance from the centre of a planet and the gravitational force falls to a quarter. At HL you meet potential, which falls with 1/r, so the same move only halves it. Faraday's law is about a rate of change: the faster the flux changes, the bigger the voltage.

In my lessons, Fields is where HL students struggle most. Electromagnetism, the part I mentioned at the top, is in this theme.

Theme E: Nuclear and quantum physics

SL: the structure of the atom, with Rutherford scattering, spectra and energy levels (E.1); radioactive decay, half-life, binding energy and mass defect (E.3); fission and nuclear reactors (E.4); and fusion and stars, including the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (E.5).

HL adds: a whole subtopic on quantum physics (E.2), with the photoelectric effect and the wave nature of matter. It also adds the Bohr model of hydrogen, and more on radioactive decay: the decay constant and activity.

The maths it needs: exponential decay. After three half-lives, one eighth of the sample is left, which is 12.5%. At HL you use the decay constant, λ = ln 2 / T½, so you need logarithms.

Stars are still on the course. The astrophysics option has gone, but fusion, the life of stars and the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram are in Theme E for everyone.

What is no longer on the syllabus

Search for IB Physics notes and you will still find pages on the old options. These are not on the current course:

  • Medical imaging, such as ultrasound and MRI
  • Fluid flow and the Reynolds number
  • Black holes and general relativity

If a revision site teaches one of these, it is using the old course.

How the syllabus reaches the exam

SL and HL each sit two papers. Paper 1 is 36% of the grade: Paper 1A is multiple choice and Paper 1B is data-based. Paper 2 is 44%. Your IA, the scientific investigation, is the other 20%. Any theme can come up on any paper, and at HL, Paper 2 covers the HL content too. There is no Paper 3 and no option any more. IB Physics Paper 1 and Paper 2 has the timings and how to approach each paper.

How to use this syllabus

  • Before your mocks: go through the subtopics, A.1 to E.5, and mark each one secure, shaky or not yet taught. Work on the shaky ones first.
  • If you are choosing between SL and HL: the "HL adds" lists are the difference. Is IB Physics HL hard? explains where the difficulty really sits.
  • If the maths is what stops you: most of the maths above is taught in IB Maths, so a gap there shows up in Physics too. Fix it first. See IB Maths tutoring.
  • For your IA: any theme can give you a good investigation. IB Physics IA topics has ideas grouped by where they sit in the syllabus.

In a first lesson with a new Physics student, I try to find out where the gap is: the physics itself, the maths underneath it, or how the answers are written.

Frequently asked questions

How many themes are in IB Physics?

Five, at both SL and HL: A Space, time and motion; B The particulate nature of matter; C Wave behaviour; D Fields; and E Nuclear and quantum physics. Each theme is split into subtopics, from A.1 to E.5.

What is the difference between IB Physics SL and HL?

HL has 240 teaching hours and SL has 150, of which 180 and 110 are theory. Everything in SL is also in HL. HL adds five subtopics (rigid body mechanics, relativity, thermodynamics, induction and quantum physics) and goes deeper in others. Both levels sit two papers, and the HL papers are longer.

Is relativity in IB Physics SL?

No. Galilean and special relativity (A.5) is HL only.

When did the IB Physics syllabus change?

The current course was first examined in 2025. The old course, with numbered topics and options, had its last exams in November 2024.

Is astrophysics still in IB Physics?

Not as an option. The new course has no options. Stars are still there: fusion and the life of stars are in Theme E (E.5) for SL and HL.
